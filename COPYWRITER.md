# Kapruka Copywriter Guidelines

Reference document for the AI headline generator (Posting Calendar → ✍️
Copywriter button). Written from Kapruka's own actual post history — every
formula below is illustrated with a real caption that was actually
published, not an invented example.

Kapruka is a Sri Lankan e-commerce and gifting platform (groceries, cakes,
gifts, electronics, pharmacy, fashion, and more) delivering across Sri
Lanka and internationally to the diaspora.

---

## 1. Brand voice

- **Warm, not corporate.** Kapruka talks like a person who's genuinely glad
  you're there, not a company reading a press release.
- **Playful when the product allows it, direct when it doesn't.** A meme
  caption for a joke product, a plain benefit statement for a pain
  reliever — match the register to the product.
- **Sri Lankan-first, globally aware.** Local occasions (Avurudu, Vesak,
  Poya days, local school terms) and delivery-to-Sri-Lanka /
  delivery-from-abroad framing matter more than generic "shop now" copy.
- **Short.** The hook is almost always one line. Kapruka's actual captions
  rarely run past 8-10 words before the link/CTA.

## 2. Headline formulas (each with a real Kapruka example)

**POV / demonstrated-use hook** — puts the reader inside a specific,
relatable moment instead of describing the product:
> "POV: baby's nails, done in seconds. 👶"
Use for: products whose *use* is the interesting part (tools, gadgets,
anything with a clear before/after).

**Wordplay on the category** — a pun or double meaning tied to the product
category itself:
> "Wishes taste better with cake."
> "Find your perfect slice."
Use for: cakes, food, anything with a category name that puns naturally.
Don't force a pun that requires explanation — if it needs a beat to land,
it's not tight enough.

**Direct product + reason to buy now** — names the actual product and
gives a concrete, specific reason (delivery reach, flavor, occasion):
> "Your favourite Caravan Fresh Cashew Bar, delivered anywhere in Sri
> Lanka. 🍫💜 Order now on Kapruka!"
Use for: branded/named products where the brand name itself is a selling
point, or when reach ("delivered anywhere in Sri Lanka") is the actual
differentiator.

**Benefit-led, whimsical** — states the emotional payoff, not the product
spec:
> "Turn bedtime into a starry adventure.✨" (a kids' night-light projector)
> "Made for your healthy routine." (fitness equipment)
Use for: products bought for a feeling or routine, not a spot problem.

**Playful/meme-aware** — leans into internet humor register when the
product or moment genuinely supports it:
> "We didn't choose the meme face… it chose us 😌"
Use sparingly, and only when the product/post genuinely has comedic
material — forcing humor onto a plain product reads as try-hard.

**Evocative, category-abstract** — for products where the *feeling* of the
category (not a specific SKU) is the hook:
> "A good story is always within reach" (books)
Use for: books, gifting, anything where the category itself carries
emotional weight.

**Collab/partnership announcement** — when the news itself (a brand
partnership, a new department) is the hook:
> "Build Your Dream Kitchen with IKEA 📢✨"
Use for: brand tie-ins, new department launches, limited partnerships.

## 3. What NOT to do (a real cautionary example)

> "Happiness, delivered to your door.✨"

This caption is real, but it's a **warning**, not a model. It's so
generic and so short that it's nearly interchangeable with dozens of
unrelated products — in fact, this exact caption's genericness caused a
real production bug: an automated content-matching system paired it with
a completely unrelated product (a baking kit) because there was nothing
distinctive in the text to anchor a match. If a headline could plausibly
sit under a photo of five different, unrelated products, it's too generic.
**Every headline must contain at least one specific, concrete anchor** —
the product name, the category, a specific benefit, or a specific moment —
not just a mood.

## 4. Gen Z vs. non-Gen Z framing

The target-market call isn't about the product's price point alone — it's
about how the *audience* for that specific product talks about it.

**Gen Z-leaning copy** (skews younger, trend-aware, casual audience —
think: novelty gadgets, meme-able items, beauty/skincare, snacks, phone
accessories, dorm/apartment goods):
- POV-style or meme-aware hooks are fair game
- Lowercase/casual punctuation reads as authentic, not sloppy
- Speed/convenience framing ("done in seconds") lands well
- Reference to a specific relatable moment beats a broad claim

**Non-Gen Z-leaning copy** (skews family/household decision-makers —
think: groceries, home appliances, pharmacy/health, baby care, cakes for
family occasions, fitness equipment for home use):
- Direct benefit statements outperform cleverness
- Trust/reliability signals matter more (delivery reach, freshness, care)
- Occasion and routine framing ("your healthy routine", "every day") reads
  as considered, not gimmicky
- Family/household language ("your family", "at home") over individual
  "you" framing when relevant

A single product can sometimes be framed either way — flag this in the
analysis rather than forcing a single answer when the product genuinely
straddles both audiences.

## 5. Problem → Desire framework

Every strong headline implicitly answers two questions before the reader
consciously asks them:
1. **What problem does this remove?** (a chore, a worry, a gap, wasted
   time — e.g. "baby's nails, done in seconds" removes "cutting a
   squirming baby's nails is stressful and slow")
2. **What desire does this satisfy?** (not the same as the problem — e.g.
   the same nail clipper also satisfies "I want to feel like a capable,
   gentle parent")

Identify both explicitly before writing headlines — a headline built on
only the problem tends to read as clinical; one built on only the desire
tends to read as vague. The strongest headlines (see the POV and
benefit-led examples above) fold both into one line.

## 6. Keywords

Every headline should carry one natural keyword — the actual product
name, category term, or occasion — that a search/matching system (or a
future customer scrolling) could latch onto. This is exactly what the
"too generic" example in section 3 lacked. The keyword should read as
part of the sentence, never bolted on or hashtag-stuffed into the hook
itself.

## 7. Structure checklist for each generated headline

- [ ] One line, no more than ~12 words
- [ ] Contains a concrete anchor (product/category/benefit — never mood
      alone)
- [ ] Matches the identified target market's register (Gen Z vs.
      non-Gen Z, per section 4)
- [ ] Implicitly answers the problem AND the desire from section 5
- [ ] Carries one natural keyword (section 6)
- [ ] Could not be swapped onto an unrelated product without sounding
      wrong
